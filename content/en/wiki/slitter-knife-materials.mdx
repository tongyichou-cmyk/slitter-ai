# Slitter Knife Materials: Tool Steel vs High-Speed Steel vs Tungsten Carbide

Knife material is the most consequential decision in slitting. Just as a Formula 1 team picks tyres for the track and conditions, slitter knives should be chosen by application, each with its own strategy. The wrong material raises slitting cost, causes poor edge quality and can lead to unplanned downtime, adding to operating cost. This guide gives tooling engineers and buyers recommendations for choosing knife material by application.

---

## Why Knife Material Matters

Four things stop a slitter knife from working properly: edges that wear too fast, edges prone to chipping (notches), plastic deformation of the edge under high load, and workpiece material sticking to the knife face. Each is governed by different material properties, so different knife materials suit different applications.

For example, a tool steel knife (SKD-11, D2, etc.) that suits mild steel may wear out before finishing a single coil of electrical steel. A carbide knife that performs perfectly on electrical steel may instead chip, or fail to deliver its potential, on carbon steel or general materials. Matching knife material to the processing conditions directly determines cost and output quality.

---

## Knife Material Properties

### Tool Steel (SKD-11, D2, 1.2379)

D2 (SKD-11) is a cold-work tool steel, hardened to 58–62 HRC. It is tougher than high-speed steel with ample wear resistance, which makes it the most all-round knife material for metal coil slitting.

**Strengths:**
- Combines toughness and hardness; tougher than high-speed steel, so less prone to chipping
- Economical and versatile: handles copper, aluminum, stainless steel, carbon steel and general metal coils
- Easy to regrind with standard wheels

**Weaknesses:**
- Lower wear resistance than high-speed steel and carbide
- On highly abrasive materials such as electrical steel, life is far below carbide

**Best applications:** Copper, aluminum, stainless steel, carbon steel and general metal coils.

---

### High-Speed Steel (SKH-51, M2, 1.3343)

High-speed steel uses tungsten, molybdenum, vanadium and cobalt to raise hardness and wear resistance. Common grades are M2 (62–64 HRC) and cobalt-bearing M42 (65–67 HRC).

**Strengths:**
- Better wear resistance than SKD-11, so longer life
- Tougher than carbide
- Relatively easy to regrind

**Weaknesses:**
- Less tough than SKD-11
- Costs more than SKD-11
- Still short of carbide life on highly abrasive materials

**Best applications:** High-volume stainless steel and nickel-based alloys.

---

### Tungsten Carbide (WC-Co)

Tungsten carbide is not a steel: it is tungsten carbide particles sintered with cobalt as the binder. Hardness is measured in HRA (Rockwell A scale) rather than HRC. Precision slitter grades run 88–92 HRA, equivalent to approximately 70–75 HRC.

**Strengths:**
- Substantially longer life than tool steel and high-speed steel on abrasive materials
- Excellent wear resistance on materials such as stainless and silicon steel
- Holds its edge for a long time, keeping edge quality consistent in batch production

**Weaknesses:**
- Brittle — chips easily under impact or a misaligned knife setup
- Requires diamond wheels and carbide-capable grinding equipment
- Several times the per-knife price of tool steel
- Very sensitive to setup: arbor runout or spacer error that causes only slight burr with tool steel will chip a carbide knife

**Best applications:** Electrical steel (silicon steel), copper foil, hardened stainless (420/440), and high-volume production where fewer changeovers pay off.

## Knife Regrinding

Tool steel and HSS knives can be reground many times before they are no longer usable. Regrinding removes material from the OD only, not the thickness. Regrinds available = (current OD − machine design minimum OD) ÷ removal per regrind. For example, a 150 mm OD knife on a machine with a 140 mm minimum OD, at 0.2 mm per regrind, allows about 50 regrinds.

**Regrind guidelines:**
- Regrind before the edge becomes visibly chipped — micro-chipping keeps growing
- Grind the whole set together so every knife ends at the same OD
- Log the OD after each regrind to track how many regrinds remain

Note: Carbide knives require diamond grinding wheels and grinding parameters suited to carbide (low feed, high-pressure coolant to prevent thermal cracking). Grinding carbide the way ordinary knives are ground causes damage such as chipping or thermal cracks. See [Slitter Knife Regrinding](/en/wiki/knife-regrinding).

## Cost Analysis: Why a More Expensive Knife Can Be Cheaper

A common blind spot in purchasing is comparing only knife prices for the same size and specification, overlooking total cost of ownership. Use the calculation below with your own knife quotes, measured knife life, and changeover and labor costs:

- **Monthly knife cost** = price per set × sets needed per month (monthly meters ÷ meters per set)
- **Monthly changeover cost** = changeovers per month × downtime and labor cost per changeover
- **Monthly regrinding cost** = sets reground per month × regrind cost per set
- **Total monthly cost** = the sum of all three

Knife prices differ by material; a longer-lasting knife needs fewer changeovers under the same conditions. The higher the volume and the more a changeover costs, the more it pays to work out which material best suits your line.

## Knife Tolerance and Edge Quality

Knife material is only one variable. The dimensional tolerance the knife is ground to affects cut-edge quality as much as the material itself.

**Thickness tolerance** determines how evenly load is shared across the knife pairs. In a 20-knife set at a thickness tolerance of ±0.003 mm, the thickest knives carry noticeably more load than the thinnest, so knives wear at different rates across the arbor and burr height varies.

**Diameter tolerance** affects cutting uniformity. Knives of unequal diameter make some pairs penetrate deeper than others, causing over-cutting and unstable cutting conditions.

Precision slitter knives are ground to thickness tolerances from ±0.005 mm to ±0.001 mm depending on the application. Where edge quality must be consistent across a wide coil—copper foil, precision stainless strip, high-grade copper alloy sheet—a thickness tolerance within ±0.001 to ±0.002 mm is recommended, and the knife tolerance must be clearly specified.

## FAQ

**Q: What is the best slitter knife material for stainless steel?**
D2 (SKD-11) tool steel for normal volumes; high-speed steel for higher volumes; tungsten carbide for even higher volumes, thicknesses above 1 mm, or 420/440 stainless.

**Q: Is tungsten carbide worth the cost?**
Usually yes for high-volume or abrasive work: fewer changeovers can outweigh the higher knife price. Compare cost per meter slit using your own knife life records.

**Q: Can tool steel, HSS and carbide knives all be reground?**
Yes. The count depends on OD: (current OD − machine minimum OD) ÷ removal per regrind. Grind the whole set together so every knife ends at the same OD. Carbide must be ground with diamond wheels on dedicated equipment.

**Q: What tolerance should I specify?**
For precision work, a thickness tolerance of ±0.001 to ±0.002 mm. Looser tolerances spread load unevenly across the set and make burr height less consistent.

**Q: Why do carbide knives chip?**
Almost always a setup issue: arbor runout, accumulated spacer error, or clearance set too tight. Avoid setup errors and keep dust and particles out from between the knives. Carbide does not tolerate the setup errors that tool steel and high-speed steel can.

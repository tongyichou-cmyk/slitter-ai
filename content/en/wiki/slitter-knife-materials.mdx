# Slitter Knife Materials: Tool Steel vs High-Speed Steel vs Tungsten Carbide

Knife material is the most consequential tooling decision in slitting. The wrong choice raises slitting cost, damages edge quality and causes unplanned downtime. This guide gives tooling engineers and buyers a basis for choosing knife material by application.

---

## Why Knife Material Matters

Four things stop a slitter knife from working properly: edge wear, edge chipping, plastic deformation of the edge under high load, and workpiece material sticking to the knife face. Each is governed by different material properties, so different knife materials suit different applications.

A knife material that lasts well on mild steel can wear out several times faster on electrical steel. A carbide knife that performs perfectly on copper foil may chip immediately on thick hot-rolled plate. Matching material to application is not optional; it directly determines cost and output quality.

---

## Knife Material Properties

### Tool Steel D2 (SKD-11)

D2 (SKD-11) is a high-carbon, high-chromium cold-work tool steel (1.5% C, 12% Cr), hardened to 58–62 HRC. It is tougher than high-speed steel with ample wear resistance, which makes it the most versatile knife material for metal coil slitting.

**Strengths:**
- Tougher than high-speed steel, so less prone to chipping
- Versatile: handles copper, stainless steel, carbon steel and general metal coils
- Easy to regrind with standard wheels

**Limitations:**
- Lower wear resistance than high-speed steel and carbide
- On highly abrasive materials such as electrical steel, life is far below carbide

**Best applications:** Copper, stainless steel, carbon steel and general metal coils.

---

### High-Speed Steel (HSS, e.g. M2, M42)

High-speed steel uses tungsten, molybdenum, vanadium and cobalt to raise hardness and wear resistance. Common grades are M2 (62–64 HRC) and cobalt-bearing M42 (65–67 HRC).

**Strengths:**
- Better wear resistance than D2 (SKD-11), so longer life
- Far tougher than carbide
- Relatively easy to regrind

**Limitations:**
- Less tough than D2 (SKD-11)
- Costs more than D2 (SKD-11)
- Does not approach carbide life on highly abrasive materials

**Best applications:** High-volume stainless steel and nickel-based alloys.

---

### Tungsten Carbide (WC-Co)

Tungsten carbide is not a steel—it is a cermet: tungsten carbide particles bonded in a cobalt matrix. Hardness is measured in HRA (Rockwell A scale) rather than HRC. Precision slitter grades run 88–92 HRA, equivalent to approximately 70–75 HRC.

**Strengths:**
- Substantially longer life than tool steel and high-speed steel on abrasive materials
- Excellent resistance to wear from hard inclusions in stainless and silicon steel
- Holds edge geometry far longer, giving consistent edge quality over long runs

**Limitations:**
- Brittle — chips easily under impact or a misaligned knife setup
- Cannot be reground on standard surface grinders; requires diamond wheels and carbide-capable equipment
- Several times the per-knife price of tool steel
- Very sensitive to setup: arbor runout or spacer error that causes only slight burr with tool steel will chip a carbide knife

**Best applications:** Electrical steel (silicon steel), copper foil, hardened stainless (420/440), lithium battery separator, and high-volume production where fewer changeovers pay off.

## Cost Per Meter: Why Carbide Can Be Cheaper

A common blind spot in purchasing is comparing knife prices without calculating total cost of ownership. Use the calculation below with your own knife quotes, measured knife life and changeover labor:

- **Monthly knife cost** = price per set × sets needed per month (monthly meters ÷ meters per set)
- **Monthly changeover cost** = changeovers per month × downtime and labor cost per changeover
- **Total monthly cost** = the sum of the two

Carbide costs more per set, but it lasts longer and, under the same conditions, needs fewer changeovers. The higher the volume and the more a changeover costs, the more carbide is favored.

## Knife Tolerance and Edge Quality

Knife material is only one variable. The dimensional tolerance the knife is ground to affects edge quality as much as the material itself.

**Thickness tolerance** determines how evenly load is shared across the knife pairs. In a 20-knife set at ±0.01 mm, the thickest knives carry noticeably more load than the thinnest, so knives wear at different rates across the arbor and burr height varies.

**Diameter tolerance** affects cutting uniformity. Knives of unequal diameter make some pairs penetrate deeper than others, so cutting is unstable.

Precision slitter knives are ground to thickness tolerances from ±0.005 mm to ±0.001 mm depending on the application. Where edge quality must be consistent across a wide coil—battery foil, precision stainless strip, high-grade copper sheet—a thickness tolerance within ±0.001 to ±0.002 mm is recommended, and the knife tolerance must be clearly specified.

## Regrinding and Reconditioning

Tool steel and HSS knives can be reground many times before replacement. Regrinding removes material from the OD only, not the thickness. Regrinds available = (current OD − machine minimum OD) ÷ removal per regrind. For example, a 150 mm OD knife on a machine with a 140 mm minimum OD, at 0.2 mm per regrind, allows about 50 regrinds.

**Regrind guidelines:**
- Regrind before the edge becomes visibly chipped — micro-chipping keeps growing
- Grind the whole set together so every knife ends at the same OD
- Log the OD after each regrind to track how many regrinds remain

Carbide knives require diamond grinding wheels and grinding parameters suited to carbide (low feed, high-pressure coolant to prevent thermal cracking). Regrinding carbide on a grinder set up for HSS causes chipping or thermal cracks. See the [knife regrinding guide](/en/wiki/knife-regrinding).

## FAQ

**Q: What is the best slitter knife material for stainless steel?**
D2 (SKD-11) tool steel for normal volumes; high-speed steel for higher volumes; tungsten carbide for even higher volumes, thicknesses above 1 mm, or 420/440 stainless.

**Q: Is tungsten carbide worth the cost?**
Usually yes for high-volume or abrasive work: fewer changeovers can outweigh the higher knife price. Compare cost per meter slit using your own knife life records.

**Q: Can tool steel and HSS knives be reground?**
Yes. The count depends on OD: (current OD − machine minimum OD) ÷ removal per regrind. Grind the whole set together so every knife ends at the same OD.

**Q: What tolerance should I specify?**
For precision work, a thickness tolerance of ±0.001 to ±0.002 mm. Looser tolerances spread load unevenly across the set and make burr height less consistent.

**Q: Why do carbide knives chip?**
Almost always a setup issue: arbor runout, accumulated spacer error, or clearance set too tight. Avoid setup errors and keep dust and particles out from between the knives. Carbide does not tolerate the setup errors that tool steel and high-speed steel can.

# Slitter Stripper Rings: Function, Materials, Shore Hardness Selection and Maintenance

One part of the knife stack does its job quietly through every run, yet stops production the moment it fails: the **stripper ring** (also called a rubber ring or elastic ring).

Stripper rings are mounted on the arbor beside the knives, with an OD slightly larger than the knife OD. After the cut, the ring deforms elastically and gently "strips" the slit strip off the knife face. This keeps the strip from sticking to the knife through static, oil film or material ductility — and then wrapping the arbor.

---

## How Stripper Rings Work

### Stripping

Slit strip tends to cling to the knife face because of:
- **Static electricity:** thin material (under 0.5 mm) picks up static at high speed
- **Oil film:** slitting oil or rust-preventive oil forms a liquid bridge
- **Ductility:** soft materials such as aluminum and copper spread slightly at the cut

The elastic face of the ring stands 2–8 mm proud of the knife OD, depending on material and line speed. As the strip leaves the stack, the ring keeps a light sideways push on it so it separates from the knife.

### Guiding

Stripper rings also guide the strip sideways, preventing lateral drift at high speed so the strips enter the separators on a stable path.

## Ring Materials

### Natural Rubber (NR)

- **Shore A hardness:** 40–70
- **Advantages:** low cost, good elasticity, suits low speeds and temperatures
- **Disadvantages:** poor oil resistance — swells and degrades in slitting oil; ages quickly above 80 °C
- **Use for:** low-speed lines (under 100 m/min), no slitting oil, thin material

### Polyurethane (PU)

- **Shore A hardness:** 50–95, the widest range
- **Advantages:** much better wear resistance than natural rubber, good oil resistance, wide hardness choice
- **Disadvantages:** costs more than natural rubber; slightly less elastic at low temperature
- **Use for:** medium- to high-speed lines (100–400 m/min) with slitting oil — the mainstream choice for metal slitting

### Neoprene (CR)

- **Shore A hardness:** 40–70
- **Advantages:** better oil resistance than natural rubber, good weathering, handles moderate temperatures (up to 120 °C)
- **Disadvantages:** less wear-resistant than polyurethane
- **Use for:** applications that need oil resistance but have light wear

### Fluoroelastomer (FKM / Viton)

- **Shore A hardness:** 60–80
- **Advantages:** high-temperature resistance (up to 200 °C), resists strong acids, alkalis and most chemicals
- **Disadvantages:** very expensive
- **Use for:** special chemical environments, such as material with corrosive coatings

## Choosing Shore A Hardness

Shore A hardness is the most important selection parameter.

**Basic principle:** harder rings hold their dimensions better at high speed; softer rings are more elastic and strip better. Find the balance between the two.

### Hardness by Material Thickness

| Material thickness | Recommended Shore A |
|--------------------|---------------------|
| Under 0.3 mm (foil) | 40–55 |
| 0.3–0.8 mm (thin) | 50–65 |
| 0.8–2.0 mm (light gauge) | 60–75 |
| 2.0–4.0 mm (medium gauge) | 70–85 |
| Over 4.0 mm (heavy gauge) | 80–95 |

### Hardness by Line Speed

| Line speed | Adjustment |
|------------|------------|
| Under 100 m/min | Lower end of the recommended range |
| 100–300 m/min | Middle of the range |
| Over 300 m/min | Upper end of the range, or 5–10 Shore A harder |

**Why:** at high speed, centrifugal force and dynamic compression can permanently deform rubber. A ring that is too soft will "flare" at speed — its OD swells unevenly and strip guidance becomes unstable.

## Ring Dimensions

### Outside Diameter

Ring OD = knife OD + extension

| Material | Recommended extension |
|----------|-----------------------|
| Thin (under 0.5 mm) | 2–4 mm |
| Light gauge (0.5–2 mm) | 3–6 mm |
| Medium gauge (2–5 mm) | 4–8 mm |
| Heavy gauge (over 5 mm) | 5–10 mm |

**Note:** more extension is not better. Too much extension:
- Pushes too hard sideways on the strip and deforms its edge
- Increases the contact area with the strip and wears the ring faster

### Bore

The ring bore must fit the arbor precisely, usually as a slight interference fit so the ring does not slip in rotation.

### Width

Ring width usually equals the width of the matching spacer, so the ring covers the whole zone where the strip contacts the stack.

## Installation Best Practice

### Before Installation

1. **Check the knife OD:** measure it with an OD micrometer and confirm the ring extension is within specification
2. **Check the arbor is clean:** no oil on the arbor surface, so the ring cannot slip
3. **Check the ring for cracks:** inspect new rings for aging cracks, tears or hardening before fitting

### Installation Steps

1. Slide the ring onto the arbor in the right orientation — symmetrical rings have none; check the direction of asymmetrical rings
2. Confirm the ring sits beside the correct knife
3. When clamping the arbor nut, make sure the ring is not over-compressed, which reduces its elastic deflection

### After Installation

Run at low speed (5–10 m/min) and watch that the strip leaves the knife face cleanly after the stack, with no tendency to wrap.

## Failure Modes

### 1. Flaring

**Symptom:** the ring OD becomes uneven; after high-speed running the outer edge is wavy or bell-mouthed
**Cause:** Shore A hardness too low, or the ring has aged and lost elasticity
**Fix:** use a harder ring; check that the working temperature is within the compound's limit

### 2. Strip keeps wrapping the arbor

**Symptom:** strip wraps the upper or lower arbor frequently, even with stripper rings fitted
**Possible causes:**
- Not enough ring extension, so the stripping force is too low
- Ring too hard for very thin material
- Strong static on the material — add static elimination

### 3. Rapid ring wear

**Symptom:** the ring OD shrinks quickly
**Cause:** large burr or slivers on the strip edge cutting the ring, or a compound with poor wear resistance
**Fix:** solve the burr problem first; switch to more wear-resistant polyurethane

### 4. Swelling or cracking

**Symptom:** the rubber face swells or cracks
**Cause:** the compound is incompatible with the slitting oil (natural rubber with mineral oil), or the temperature exceeds its limit
**Fix:** switch to oil-resistant polyurethane or neoprene; check the working temperature

## When to Replace

### Measured Criteria

| Indicator | Replace when |
|-----------|--------------|
| OD wear | Extension reduced by more than half (for example from 6 mm to 3 mm or less) |
| Hardness loss | More than 10 Shore A below a new ring |
| Out-of-round | TIR over 0.5 mm |

### Visual Criteria

- Cracks deeper than 1 mm in the rubber face
- Widespread swelling of the rubber (volume up more than 15%)
- Permanent compression set on the ring faces (loss of parallelism)

### Preventive Replacement

Keep a usage record for each ring set (meters slit per month) and replace complete sets preventively every 3–6 months, depending on usage, to avoid unplanned stoppages.

## FAQ

**Q: What happens if the hardness is wrong?**
Too soft: the ring swells at speed and guidance becomes unstable. Too hard: not enough elasticity to strip thin material, so it wraps anyway.

**Q: Different choices for stainless and carbon steel?**
Stainless leaves more burr and wears rings faster — use polyurethane, about 5–10 Shore A harder.

**Q: Can stripper rings be reground with the knives?**
No. Remove them before regrinding and check the extension after reassembly.

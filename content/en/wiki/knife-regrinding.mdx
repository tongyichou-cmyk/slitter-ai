# Slitter Knife Regrinding: When to Regrind, Grinding Standards and Knife Life Management

A slitter knife is not a consumable — it is a **precision tool that can be restored many times**. With good regrinding practice, a carbide knife can go through dozens of regrind cycles before it reaches its minimum usable diameter.

Regrinding too late, poor grinding practice or the wrong equipment shortens knife life sharply and can scrap knives that still had years of service left. This guide covers when to regrind, how to grind, and how to manage knife life.

---

## Why Regrind Instead of Buying New Knives?

### Cost Considerations

A well-managed knife can be reground many times, and each regrind costs far less than a new knife, so a planned regrinding program substantially lowers the lifetime tooling cost per knife.

Regrinding cost depends on the knife's outer diameter, bore, thickness and material, so it is quoted against the actual dimensions.

### Does Regrinding Affect Knife Quality?

A correctly reground knife cuts as well as a new one. When it does not, the cause is usually:
- Regrinding too late, so a large removal is needed
- The wrong grinding wheel, causing heat damage
- Not checking that the whole set has the same OD after grinding

**Key principle:** Set a regrind trigger and regrind **before** burr reaches it. Do not wait for a customer complaint.

### Indicator 2: Cutting Force (Drive Current)

As knives wear, cutting force rises and so does the slitter drive current. Record a baseline current for each line and material, and schedule a regrind when current rises noticeably above it.

### Indicator 3: Visual Inspection

Inspect the cutting edge with a 10× loupe:
- **Wear land:** an even, reflective band on the edge (normal wear) → schedule a regrind
- **Chipping:** notches in the edge → regrind immediately; chips grow if the knife stays in service
- **Rolled edge:** the edge has folded over → schedule a regrind

### Indicator 4: Meters Slit

Build a knife life record for each material, and predict the next regrind from your own history. Track meters or tonnes slit per knife set and per arbor position, together with the burr height at the time of each regrind.

## Grinding Standards

### D2 (SKD-11) Tool Steel and High-Speed Steel Knives

**Wheel selection:**
- Abrasive: CBN (cubic boron nitride) or white aluminum oxide (WA)
- Grit: 120–180 for finishing, 80–120 for semi-finishing
- Bond: vitrified (V) or resin (B)
- Grade: medium-soft to medium-hard (H–K)

**Grinding parameters:**
- Wheel speed: 20–35 m/s
- Infeed per pass: 0.005–0.01 mm finishing, 0.02–0.05 mm roughing
- Coolant: generous flow to prevent local overheating

**Quality requirements:**
- Face roughness Ra ≤ 0.4 μm
- OD tolerance: ±0.002 mm
- Face parallelism: within 0.002 mm

### Carbide Knives

Tungsten carbide (WC-Co) **must be ground with diamond wheels**. Conventional wheels cannot grind it properly.

**Wheel selection:**
- Abrasive: synthetic diamond (D)
- Grit: 120–150 for finishing, 80–100 for roughing
- Bond: resin (B); avoid vitrified bonds because of thermal stress

**Grinding parameters:**
- Wheel speed: 20–25 m/s, slightly lower than for tool steel
- Infeed per pass: 0.002–0.005 mm finishing, 0.01–0.02 mm roughing
- Coolant: **mandatory and generous**, with high-pressure coolant aimed at the grinding contact zone

**Carbide-specific precautions:**
- Never grind carbide without coolant — it causes thermal cracking ("burn")
- Do not grind carbide on an ordinary tool steel grinder: the wheel is wrong and the coolant system is often inadequate
- After grinding, inspect for micro-cracks at 20× magnification or higher

### How Much to Remove

**Key principle:** Remove just enough to restore a sharp edge — no more.

| Wear level | Suggested removal per regrind (OD) |
|------------|------------------------------------|
| Light (burr just over the trigger) | 0.03–0.08 mm |
| Moderate (clear wear land) | 0.08–0.15 mm |
| Heavy (chipping or severe wear) | 0.15–0.30 mm or more |

**Why regrind early:** small removals → more regrinds → longer knife life. A knife left until heavily worn needs a much larger removal each time, so it can be reground far fewer times.

## Checking After Regrinding

### Grind the Whole Set Together

Regrinding removes material from the OD only, not the thickness. Grind the whole set together so every knife ends at the same OD, then check that the OD difference is ≤ 0.002 mm, so penetration is the same across each pair.

**Do not regrind only some of the knives.** If one knife is more worn and needs a larger removal, grind the whole set down to the same OD.

### Record Dimensions After Every Regrind

Record:
- Regrind date
- OD before and after, to calculate the removal
- Knife set ID

Without this record you cannot calculate how many regrinds remain, and a knife can unexpectedly reach its minimum diameter.

## Minimum Usable Diameter

Knife life is set by the **minimum usable diameter**, not by a number of regrinds.

What limits the minimum diameter:
1. **The machine's minimum knife OD for the arbor:** below it, the contact with the spacers becomes insufficient
2. **Clearance geometry:** as OD shrinks, spacer sizes must change to keep the correct clearance, and beyond a certain range the spacers can no longer compensate
3. **Side face removal:** some regrinding removes material from the side faces as well; accumulated side removal can affect how the knife fits the arbor

**Recommendation:** Confirm the minimum usable OD for your machine with the machine builder or knife supplier, and track the remaining allowance in your regrind records.

## Five Criteria for Evaluating a Regrinding Service

### 1. Equipment

- **Tool steel knives:** high-precision CNC cylindrical grinders with spindle runout below 0.001 mm
- **Carbide knives:** diamond wheel grinders with an adequate coolant system

Avoid services that regrind precision slitter knives on manual grinders — manual setup and infeed control cannot hold ±0.002 mm.

### 2. Measurement

The service should have:
- OD micrometers with 0.001 mm resolution
- Electronic face parallelism measurement
- A measurement report for every regrind

### 3. Whole-Set Regrinding

Confirm that the service **grinds the whole set together** rather than grinding each knife separately, so every knife in the set ends at the same OD.

### 4. Material Identification

The service must identify the knife material correctly and use the matching wheel. Grinding carbide with a tool steel wheel is the most common source of regrinding damage.

### 5. Lead Time and Packaging

Reground precision knives must travel in foam or rigid protective holders so they cannot knock against each other and chip. Avoid services that return knives loose in plastic bags.

## Building a Knife Life Management System

### Knife Numbering

Give every knife a unique ID and track its full life:
- Purchase date
- Initial OD and thickness
- Each regrind: date, OD before and after
- Materials slit
- Retirement date (minimum diameter reached, or unrepairable chipping)

### Planned Regrinding

Schedule regrinds from:
1. Typical knife life per material, in meters or tonnes
2. The monthly production plan
3. The predicted next regrind date, so a spare set is ready in advance

### Spare Knife Sets

**Minimum rule:** Always keep at least one complete, freshly reground spare set, so production continues while the working set is out for regrinding.

## FAQ

**Q: How many times can a knife be reground?**
With a 150 mm starting OD, a 140 mm minimum OD and 0.2 mm removed per regrind, about 50 times in theory. Regrinding early keeps removals small and maximizes the count.

**Q: Is a reground knife as good as new?**
Yes, if reground correctly. Poor results usually come from heat damage or the wrong wheel.

**Q: Can I regrind just one knife of a pair?**
Not recommended. Grind the whole set together to the same OD (difference ≤ 0.002 mm).

**Q: How do I evaluate a regrinding service?**
Check for CNC grinders, diamond wheels for carbide, OD measurement reports, whole-set regrinding and protective packaging.
